# outline

## 1. NEWS — the instrument just arrived (0-8s)

meta dropped TRIBEv2. the headline is: you can now predict fMRI brain scans from any stimulus without a human in the scanner. that's the news. don't oversell it — just state the capability and let the viewer feel the weight of what that unlocks.

## 2. BACKGROUND — what is fMRI, what is the brain doing (8-20s)

ground it in the body. fMRI = where blood moves = where energy is being spent = what the brain is working on. 20,000 measurement points across the cortex. show the classical stuff: food lights up reward, a face lights up fusiform, a threat lights up amygdala. "you can now replicate decades of neuroscience experiments in simulation." this is the credibility beat — the model isn't making things up, it's recovering known phenomena.

## 3. THE UNLOCK — predicting brain response to experiences, then optimizing for it (20-35s)

pivot from "replicate old experiments" to "do new ones." the unlock isn't just observation — it's that if you can predict brain activation from a stimulus, you can go the other direction: design the stimulus that produces the brain state you want.

### 3.1 example: cluttered vs beautiful UX (20-28s)

split screen: phone recording of cluttered UX flow on left, brain activation on right, animated together. motor cortex grinding, frontal overload, reward structures dark. frustration. unpredictability. the brain is spending oxygen and glucose just trying to figure out where things are.

then the flip: same split, beautiful UX. natural gestures. the brain settles into something calmer — less frontal load, reward structures warming up, somatosensory and motor cortex in a relaxed rhythm. not just "effortless" in the design language sense — LITERALLY less metabolically expensive to process. the brain gets into a more coherent state. you feel more comfortable bc your brain IS more comfortable.

### 3.2 example: predictability vs unpredictability (28-35s)

maybe: a navigation flow where buttons are where you expect them vs one where the layout shifts between screens. or an onboarding that reveals complexity progressively vs one that dumps everything at once. the key observation: predictability isn't boring — it's CHEAP for the brain. unpredictability isn't engaging — it's TAXING. the brain has to maintain more hypotheses, burn more energy, keep tighter error-correction loops. that's not a vibe — it's measurable cortical activation.

## 4. THE DEEPER POINT — it's not about any one region, it's the trajectory (35-42s)

this is where your thesis voice comes in. the important thing isn't "stimulate the reward center" or "minimize frontal activation." that's the reductive trap. what matters is the WHOLE trajectory — the shape of the causal structure as it iterates along the thalamocortical loop, the dynamics of prediction and control that constitute what it's like to use this thing. consciousness isn't a region. it's a pattern of maintenance. good UX supports that pattern. bad UX disrupts it.

this is also where you preempt the "isn't this just manipulation?" objection: we don't want to recreate the pitfalls of advertising or pharma. we're not trying to hijack reward circuits. we're trying to design interfaces that respect the dynamics of cognition — that make the maintenance cheaper, not that make the exploitation more efficient.

## 5. WHAT I BUILT — HIX signals + the closed loop (42-50s)

the builder turn. eight brain-derived signals. the closed-loop diagram. "not did users click, but did users' brains thrive." and the trust constraint — without it, you converge on dark patterns. the ethics is in the math.

## 6. CLOSE — open source + the thesis (50-60s)

open source. public capability. not a moat.

and then the landing: bad UX is measurably violent to cognition. and now we can see it.
